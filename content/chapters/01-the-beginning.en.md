*Bandwar, March 2008*

There was no doctor at the hospital that night. There had not been one for weeks — perhaps months, no one remembers exactly. So my mother was carried across the dark fields to a neighbour's house, where a small room had been cleared, and where a woman from Begusarai[^1] had agreed to come.

The room was narrow. Its ceiling was low. There was a single bulb hanging by a wire, and on the wall, someone had pasted a calendar from the year before.

Inside that room, my mother lay on a mattress spread on the floor, breathing through her pain. Outside, in the courtyard, my father sat alone on a wooden stool, waiting.

He did not know what to do with his hands.

---

When the child was born — when I was born — the woman from Begusarai wiped her hands on a cloth and went to find my grandmother. Dadi[^2] was sitting near the door, looking at the ground.

*"Chachi,"* the woman said, in her flat, news-bearing tone. *"Pota hua hai."*

A grandson.

Dadi did not smile. She did not weep. She simply stood up, walked to the neighbour's veranda, and dialled my father's number.

She told him three words. She did not have to tell him more.

---

My father was not in the village that night. He was in a nearby town, at a relative's house, for some work I have never been able to clarify.

When the phone rang, he picked it up.

He listened.

And then he went quiet.

Not the quiet of shock. Not the quiet of joy. Just quiet. The kind of quiet a man enters when he does not yet know what he is supposed to feel.

My mother has told me this story many times. She says she could not see his face that night, because he was not in the room — but she has imagined it, over the years, again and again. She has imagined him holding the phone to his ear and saying nothing. She has imagined him standing there, in that other town, in another house, in someone else's lamplight, hearing that he had a son.

*"What did he say?"* I once asked her.

She thought for a moment.

*"Kuch nahi,"* she said. *"He said nothing."*

---

When my father finally arrived — late that night — he came to the room where my mother lay and I lay beside her. He did not speak. He did not weep. He simply sat at the edge of the mattress and looked at me for a long time.

Then, very slowly, he reached for me and held me against his chest.

Not for long. A minute, perhaps two. But my mother remembers it as the first moment — the only moment in those first days — when he allowed himself to feel something.

He was twenty-six years old. He had a wife, two daughters, and now a son. He had a house that would soon be divided. He had a job that did not pay enough. He had no idea, that night, what any of it would come to.

He just held me.

Then he handed me back and went outside again, and sat on the wooden stool, and looked at the sky.

---

For six days, I had no name.

This is not unusual in our village, but it is worth saying: a child can live for a week without a name. It is not a crisis. It is not even a matter of much urgency. The child will eat. The child will sleep. The child will be held by many hands, and every hand will call the child something different — a sound, a softness, a word.

For those six days, I was simply *"the new one."*

Then, on the seventh day, the naming ceremony was held. Pandit ji[^3] arrived in the morning — a thin, quiet man with a cloth bag containing his books. He sat cross-legged on the floor of the main room, opened the horoscope my mother had kept ready, and studied it for a long time.

Then he looked up.

*"N se rakhna,"* he said. *"It will be auspicious."*

A name beginning with the letter N.

Suggestions began to circulate around the room. Nishant. Chinmay. Naman. Each was spoken, considered, set aside.

My mother said nothing.

She was sitting in the corner, holding me. Her hair was loose. There were still circles under her eyes from the birth. She had not slept properly in a week.

Then she spoke.

*"R se rakhna hai,"* she said. *"It will be with R."*

The room went silent. Pandit ji looked at her, then at my father, then back at her.

*"R?"* he said.

*"R,"* my mother said.

No one argued with her. No one ever argued with my mother when she had decided something. But no one understood, either. Why R? What was wrong with N?

The answer came later, when I asked her. Years later, when I was old enough to ask.

*"Because my name is Rajani,"* she said. *"I thought — if the boy has the same letter as his mother, it will be a good thing."*

---

The pandit left that day without giving me a name. He had been overruled, and he knew it, and he said nothing more about it. My mother had made up her mind. But she had not yet decided *which* R.

Ravi? Rahul? Raj? Rohit?

Days passed. The name did not come.

And in the meantime — in that space left open by the absence of a name — something else slipped in.

---

I was a difficult baby. My mother says this gently, but she says it. I cried all night. I cried until my throat was hoarse. My sisters would cover their ears with their pillows. My father would sit up in the dark, not knowing what to do.

Then one night, a neighbour — a young man who lived two houses away — heard me from the street. He had been walking home, he said, and he had heard the sound from far off, and he had thought: *what is that?* And he had come in.

He stood in the doorway and looked at me for a moment. Then he laughed.

*"Arre,"* he said. *"This one's a little bell. Always ringing."*

*Ghantee.*[^4]

A small bell.

Everyone laughed. My mother laughed. My father, for the first time in days, laughed.

And from that night, they called me Ghantee. My mother called me Ghantee. My father called me Ghantee. Dadi — who would be gone within the year — called me Ghantee. Nani,[^5] and the neighbours, and the whole town.

The name stayed with me for years. Long after I was given a real name. Long after I began to understand that a name is a small thing and a large thing at once.

---

My mother, when I was small, used to frighten me on purpose.

This is a strange thing to write, but it is true. When I cried too much — when nothing else worked — she would stand above me and contort her face, and lower her voice, and say:

*"Bhoot[^6] aa gaya! Bhoot aa gaya!"*

The ghost is here.

And I would go silent. Instantly. My small body would freeze, and my eyes would go wide, and I would stare up at her as if she were the most terrifying thing in the world.

Which, in that moment, she was.

And then — after a few seconds, once the silence had held — she would gather me into her lap and feed me milk, and hum, and stroke my head.

Fear and love, from the same hands. In the same minute.

I have thought about this often, as an adult. I do not think my mother was being cruel. I think she was a young woman alone in a divided house, with three children and a husband who could not speak to her, and no help, and no rest, and she was doing what generations of mothers before her had done — using whatever tool worked.

But I also think it is the first thing I learned about love. That it can be terrible. That it can arrive dressed as something else. That it can frighten you into silence and then hold you while you weep.

I was six days old and I did not know my own name. But I already knew this: that the person who frightened me most in the world was also the person who would not let me go.

---

I do not remember any of this. I was too young. I was too small. I remember the later things — the house, the fields, my sisters, the sound of my mother's voice — but I do not remember Bandwar. I do not remember Dadi. I do not remember the room where I was born.

What I remember is what I was told. And what I was told, for most of my childhood, was very little.

It was only in 2024 — when I was in tenth grade, sixteen years old, studying for exams that would decide the next decade of my life — that my mother told me the whole story.

Not in one sitting. Never in one sitting. In fragments, over many weeks, when the moment was right. She told me about the night of my birth, and about Seema aunty's house, and about Dadi hearing the news first. She told me about the six days without a name, and the pandit, and the R.

And she told me something else, something I had not known: that Dadi had died when I was eleven months old.

I had known this, of course. I had known that Dadi had died when I was small. But I had not known the other thing. The thing that has stayed with me.

There is a photograph. Somewhere in an old album. From the day Dadi died.

In the photograph, I am lying beside her. Eleven months old. Sleeping.

I do not know who took the photo. I do not know why. But it exists.

I found it in 2019. I was in sixth grade. I was looking through an album for something else entirely, and there it was — my tiny body beside my grandmother's body, and something in the scene that I did not then understand.

I looked at it for a long time.

I did not know what to do with it.

So I put it back in the album and closed the album and went outside to play.

It is only now, as I write this, that I understand what I was looking at. My grandmother, who heard the news of my birth before anyone else. My grandmother, who chose my nickname before I had a name. My grandmother, who died four months before I would have remembered her.

In the photograph, I am asleep beside her, and she is already cold.

I do not know if she ever held me.

I will never know.

But I know this: somewhere in the six days between my birth and my naming, in that nameless, formless time — she held me. I am certain of it. A woman who heard the news first would have held me.

That is what I choose to believe.

---

Two days after I was born, my name was finally decided. Ravi Raj. It was my mother's choice, and no one contested it.

But by then, the name was already late. The town had already decided what to call me.

*Ghantee.*

The little bell.

[^1]: A city in Bihar, India.

[^2]: Paternal grandmother.

[^3]: A Hindu priest.

[^4]: A small bell — Ravi Raj's childhood nickname.

[^5]: Maternal grandmother.

[^6]: Ghost — used playfully to scare the child.
